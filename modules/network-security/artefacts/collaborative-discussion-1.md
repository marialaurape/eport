# Collaborative Discussion 1
## Digital Enterprise and Cybersecurity

### Initial Post

I consider a fully digital enterprise to be one where digital technologies are integrated into core business processes rather than simply used as supporting tools. Operations, communication, customer services and decision-making may depend on technologies such as cloud services, ERP systems, data analytics and e-commerce. Doroiman and Sîrghi (2024) similarly distinguish basic digital adoption from more comprehensive digital transformation, finding that basic digital intensity alone does not necessarily produce significant economic benefits.

From a cybersecurity perspective, this dependence creates additional risks. As more systems, data and services become interconnected, the attack surface increases and a cyber incident can directly affect business operations. Cloud services, digital identities and third-party platforms can introduce vulnerabilities, while ransomware, compromised credentials or service disruption may have significant operational consequences. Cybersecurity therefore becomes part of business resilience rather than only an IT responsibility.

For a bricks-and-mortar SME becoming digital, I believe the main challenge is that digital adoption can develop faster than security maturity. Doroiman and Sîrghi (2024) highlight skills, financial resources and external support as important factors affecting SME digitalisation. An SME may therefore adopt cloud services, online sales and other digital platforms without having equivalent resources for vulnerability management, access control, monitoring or incident response.

For this reason, cybersecurity should develop alongside digital transformation rather than being introduced afterwards. Digitalisation can create significant opportunities, but its benefits increasingly depend on an organisation's ability to secure and maintain the systems on which it becomes dependent.

### Peer Response 1

I agree that the transition itself can create significant security risks for SMEs, particularly when new technologies are adopted faster than the organisation can develop the skills and processes needed to secure them.

One way to reduce this risk would be to integrate basic security controls into the digitalisation process from the beginning. For example, cloud adoption should include multi-factor authentication, appropriate access controls and configuration reviews, while regular vulnerability assessments can identify weaknesses as new systems are introduced.

I also think a risk-based approach is particularly important for SMEs. With limited budgets and expertise, they may not be able to implement the same security capabilities as larger organisations, so prioritising critical systems and using managed security services where necessary could provide a more realistic approach.

This complements Doroiman and Sîrghi’s (2024) argument that effective digital transformation requires more than simply adopting technology.

### Peer Response 2

I agree with your point that greater digital integration can allow a compromise in one system to affect other parts of the organisation. This becomes particularly important when cloud services, endpoints and third-party platforms are highly interconnected.

In addition to prevention, I think SMEs should focus on limiting the impact of a successful compromise. Network segmentation, least-privilege access and multi-factor authentication can reduce opportunities for lateral movement, while tested backups and a basic incident response plan can improve recovery from incidents such as ransomware.

This is particularly relevant for SMEs because, as Doroiman and Sîrghi (2024) highlight, digitalisation is affected by limitations in skills and resources. A smaller organisation may therefore benefit more from prioritising a few effective controls around its most critical systems than attempting to implement a complex security programme immediately.

### Summary Post

My initial view of a fully digital enterprise focused primarily on the extent to which digital technologies are embedded in core business processes. After considering the wider discussion, I would retain this definition but place greater emphasis on dependency: the more an organisation relies on interconnected digital services for its operations, the more cybersecurity becomes directly linked to business resilience.

The peer discussions also reinforced that digital transformation does not necessarily produce equivalent security maturity. Doroiman and Sîrghi (2024) identify skills and resources as important factors affecting digitalisation, which is particularly relevant to SMEs. However, the discussion highlighted that limited resources do not simply create a need for more security controls; they create a need for prioritisation. SMEs may achieve greater value by identifying critical systems and implementing proportionate controls rather than attempting to replicate the security programmes of larger enterprises.

Another important point emerging from the discussion was the need to consider what happens after preventative controls fail. My initial post emphasised risks such as ransomware, compromised credentials and service disruption, but the peer discussions extended this towards resilience and recovery. Controls such as network segmentation, least privilege and multi-factor authentication can reduce the likelihood or impact of compromise, while tested backups and incident response procedures support containment and recovery.

Overall, I now see secure digital transformation as a continuous risk-management process rather than a separate technical stage. Security should develop alongside new cloud services, platforms and business processes, with controls selected according to organisational risk, resources and operational dependencies. Digital maturity should therefore not be measured only by how extensively technology is adopted, but also by whether the organisation can securely operate, monitor and recover the digital systems on which it increasingly depends.
